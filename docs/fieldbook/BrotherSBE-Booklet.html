Start here

Ship AI-built backend and data work without becoming the full-time reviewer of it

One governed workflow carries a change from the sentence somebody says out loud to the evidence a human reads before merging. Three mechanisms hold it together: design before code, own work before parallelism, evidence before done.

Design before code. Evidence before done.

Small teams can now produce far more engineering than they can personally verify. BrotherSBE is the workflow that closes that gap without putting your best person permanently on review duty.

AI increased your team's output. It also created a new job

The writing got faster. The deciding did not. Every change your team ships still owes somebody an answer to the same six questions, and none of them is answered by reading the diff more carefully.

  • Was the right thing designed? Not the thing that was asked for in a hurry on Monday morning, the thing the work is actually for.
  • Did two agents change the same ground? Two workers on the same file, in the same hour, with nobody holding the pen.
  • Did the migration actually reverse? Not whether a rollback was written. Whether the reverse leg was ever run.
  • Does the number reconcile independently? Or was it confirmed by re-running the query that produced it.
  • Do the tests prove the requirement? Or only the code that happened to get written.
  • Who reviewed the part that actually changed? A contract, a schema, a secret path, a production config, each wants a different pair of eyes.

On a team of two hundred, those questions get absorbed by process. On a team of five, they land on one person, and it is always the same person: the one senior enough to be trusted with them. That is the new job nobody applied for. It arrives quietly, it never appears on a roadmap, and it scales directly with how much of your engineering the agents write.

Output went up. Your most expensive person became the bottleneck for whether any of it is true.

Where does a change go, from the first sentence to the merge decision?

Three mechanisms: design before code, own work before parallelism, evidence before done.

Does every change now owe paperwork?

No, and this is the part that decides whether a team keeps using it. The amount you have to write scales with what the change can break, and the lowest tier owes nothing at all. A rename, a log line, a copy fix and a test tweak pass through with no ceremony. Rigour is spent where the money is. How much of your work lands in that lowest tier is the first thing worth measuring, in your own first week, on your own last month of requests, because it has never been measured on your estate and this booklet will not quote you a number for it.

What it does for the seat you sit in

Staff or Principal engineer: stop being the compression layer for everyone else's context

The reasoning you would otherwise repeat in four review comments gets written once, before implementation, as a decision with its rejected alternatives and the condition that would flip it. Specialist reviewers are routed deterministically from the diff, so the question of who should look at a contract change stops being answered with whoever is free. It names the roles the diff calls for and adds nobody to anything: the routing is yours to act on, which is also why it can never quietly hand your name to a queue.

Backend engineer: the contract change stops being a surprise to whoever consumes it

Boundaries, systems of record and the shape of what you are changing are settled before the implementation, not reconstructed from the diff afterwards. Your own work is claimed and scoped before it starts, so a parallel worker heading for the same file is something a human can see in a registry rather than something you discover in a merge conflict.

Data engineer: the grain gets argued before the SQL exists

Every entity has to name a system of record and every relationship has to state a cardinality before the design passes, which forces the argument that would otherwise happen months later, during an incident, with five candidate stores and no agreed answer. The check confirms the answer was written, never that it is right, and that is the point: it puts the question in front of the people who can settle it while settling it is still cheap.

QA engineer: your checks become the thing that decides done

A registered check leaves a receipt minted by the runner that executed it, recording what ran, its exit code and how long it took, rather than a claim somebody typed. A registered check with no receipt is reported by name as absent, and absent is never reported as a pass. What it cannot do is notice a check that nobody registered, which is why the register is the part of this that belongs to you.

Technical BA: what you wrote survives the handover to people who were asleep when you wrote it

Purpose, process and verification are written before code and committed in one directory beside it, so the next person reads the intent rather than guessing at it; no machine reads those three for meaning, and they are not meant to, because the reader is a person. Work crosses a shift boundary as a scoped brief carrying acceptance criteria, dependencies and stop conditions, and comes back as a handover record bound to a specific commit.

Platform engineer: the enforcement stays yours, and it finally gets something worth enforcing

The workflow separates writer and reviewer roles structurally, keeps reviewers read only, and under strict mode can block the CI gates you configure. It ships no branch protection and no merge checks of its own, so the enforcement stays yours: it produces the verdict, your platform decides what a verdict stops. A merge rule still reads a name and a conclusion and cannot tell a failure from an absence, which is exactly why the verdict prints that difference by name for the human who reads it.

What it will claim, and what it refuses to claim

Everything in this booklet is checkable, and where the floor is low the booklet says how low rather than hiding it. That is a design position, not modesty: a team that believes a gate proves more than it does will trust it at exactly the wrong moment.

What it proves

That the design artifacts exist, in the required shape, on the commit under review. That the risk tier was computed from the five answers rather than chosen by whoever was in a hurry. That a named check ran, on a specific commit, and left a receipt carrying its exit code and its duration. That a declared approval carries a host verified signature naming somebody other than the author. That work crossed a boundary to a named receiver who could have refused it.

What it refuses to claim

No percentages, because nothing here has been independently measured across teams. It does not prevent incidents, does not reduce defects by any number, and does not make a small team perform like a larger one. It never merges, approves or deploys, and it cannot see a change that needed a design and declared none. It cannot prove that the design came before the code, because a dossier written afterwards passes the same checks. It cannot stop two people editing the same file: it makes the collision legible to a human rather than refusing it.

Three worked examples of the same honesty, each of which is a limit the workflow prints on its own runs:

  • A skipped check is not a pass. A job skipped by a condition still reports success to a merge rule, which compares a name to a conclusion and has no opinion about whether anything was examined. That is reported here as absent evidence, by name.
  • An unsigned approval is not an approval. Without host verified signatures, a declared approval reads as absent rather than as a pass. On a CI agent with no reviewer keys imported, nothing passes, and the booklet says so before you find out in a pipeline.
  • A second derivation is not proof of independence. The numbers gate proves that two derivations differ in text and that two transcribed figures match. It runs no query of its own. Two queries over the same broken view still agree, and that limit is printed on every run.

Absent evidence is NO-DATA. NO-DATA is never a pass.

Never a pass, and on its own never a block either. It prints. What a printed verdict is allowed to stop stays a decision your platform makes, on rules you already own. The point of the distinction is that a human reading the verdict can tell a failure from a silence, which no merge rule can do for them.

The trust tab later in this booklet carries the full mechanics: how receipts are bound to a commit, why reviewers are read only, how reviewer routing is derived from the diff rather than assigned, what strict mode can and cannot block, and the register of known limits with the gap printed beside each entry.

Try it on the change you would be most embarrassed to get wrong

Not the toy. The one you would not want to explain in a post mortem. That is the change where the workflow either earns its place in an afternoon or does not, and either answer is worth having quickly.

  • Staff or Principal engineer. The decision you have now explained three times in three different threads, that still is not written down anywhere a new joiner can find it.
  • Backend engineer. The contract change with two consumers you are fairly sure about and one you are not.
  • Data engineer. The figure that appears in a board pack, that nobody has re-derived by a genuinely different route since the week it was written.
  • QA engineer. The suite that is green today, which you could not prove actually opened a file.
  • Technical BA. The request whose blast radius was settled in a meeting nobody minuted.
  • Platform engineer. The required check that would still report success if the job it runs were deleted tomorrow.

Pick one. Run it through the ten stages once. Then read the evidence the workflow produced and decide whether you would have had it otherwise.

Six short sections. What is going wrong on teams shipping backend and data work with AI, what stops it immediately, what stops it coming back, and the one incident that started all of this.

Your team did not agree to become the reviewer of record for a machine

The more engineering you hand to Claude, the more of your week goes to deciding whether what came back is true.

Your team is small. Two engineers, maybe five, maybe eight. The painkiller: stop taking the agent's word for it

Four things stop hurting in the first week.

A small team. More output. Less taking it on faith.

The design checks, the four hard gates and the silent-failure linter each ship with named tests and run in CI on Linux and Windows. The tier computation is re-derived from the diff and compared against what was declared. Act five prints the whole register with the gap beside each entry.

The medicine: stop the same class of wrong from becoming normal

Catching this week's bad migration is worth something. It is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is that four failures keep coming back on backend and data teams, every one of them silent, and each is closed by a different phase of the same loop.

The number that agreed with itself

A daily revenue figure had been in the board pack for five months. Finance queried it once, in March, and an engineer checked it by re-running the model. The model returned the same number. Everyone moved on.

In August somebody joined orders to order lines to answer a different question, and the total came out lower. Not by a rounding error. By eleven per cent.

The model had been fanning out on a join since the week it was written. Re-running it had confirmed nothing, because the check and the thing being checked were the same query.

Nobody was careless. The engineer who checked it did exactly what checking looks like. The failure was structural: a second derivation that walks the same path is not a second derivation, and nothing in the pipeline knew the difference.

A check that reads the same path as the work is not a check. It is the work, run twice.

This is what the numbers gate exists for, and it is also the clearest example of an honest limit. The gate demands a second derivation whose text genuinely differs from the first, re-run to zero drift against a pinned snapshot. It cannot read which tables each derivation touches. Two queries over the same broken view still agree, and the gate still passes them.

That floor is worth having and it is worth knowing the height of. A team that believes the gate proves independence will trust it at exactly the wrong moment, which is why the limit is printed on every run rather than kept in a footnote.

Designs, checks, proves

Three words on the cover, and each is a different mechanism.

PillarWhat it means concretelyWhat enforces it
DesignsSix phases run in order before code: purpose, process, architecture, data, expression, verification. The output is a dossier of at most eight files in one directory.Five design checks, blocking in CI: artifacts, decision record, data model, diagrams, and no file left as the shipped template.
ChecksFour failure classes get a structural gate, chosen because each fails silently: numbers, migration, approval, and whether a check actually ran.sbe_gate.py, advisory in a session, blocking under --strict in CI.
ProvesA claim is backed by a receipt naming what ran, its exit code and its duration, or it is labelled UNVERIFIED next to the item.The evidence store and the ran gate. A check that took no time did not run.

Underneath all three sits one rule that is easier to state than to find elsewhere.

Absent evidence is NO-DATA. NO-DATA is never a pass.

Three verdicts, not two. Something passed. Something failed. And something was never there to read, which is reported by name, and which never decides an exit code in either direction. Most tooling has two states and quietly sorts "did not run" into the good one. That collapse is where wrong results live.

Enforced at construction rather than by convention: a check is refused registration if it declares PASS as its empty state, and a meta-test enumerates the registries rather than a written list, so a check added next year is covered on the day it is registered.

What it is, and three things it is not

BrotherSBE is a Claude Code plugin that behaves like a senior backend and data engineering colleague. You describe the outcome in plain language. It decides the method, works in the order the engineering actually runs, and refuses to call anything finished until a check has run and left a receipt.

It is not a prompt pack. A skill pack changes how the model writes. This changes what has to exist on disk before a merge is allowed. A rule in a prompt is not a control; a control is a check that runs.

It is not a linter. A linter reads the diff, which means it arrives after the grain was chosen and the boundary was drawn. There is a linter inside this, hunting the patterns that swallow errors, but it is one of the smaller parts.

It is not a replacement for your reviewers. It routes reviewers deterministically from the diff and gives each a narrow brief, and every one of them is read-only by construction. It does not approve anything. Nothing here detects that a change needed a human approval in the first place.

It is the domain specialist sibling of BrotherMode, the general orchestrator. They share a spine and diverge on scope. BrotherSBE is standalone: clone it and it works with nothing else installed.

Act two

One team, one sprint

Four people, five days. Finance says the daily revenue figure disagrees with the ledger. Fixing it turns out to mean a data model, a schema migration, a service contract and a test suite nobody trusts. Every command appears where the sprint needs it, not before.

The team: Priya on the warehouse, Tom on the service, Adaeze on infrastructure and release, and Marc, the Technical BA who owns what the business actually asked for. Nobody has a spare week.

Monday

The work is sized before it is scoped

Marc opens the session and says what happened, in the words Finance used.

/brothersbe:start

One question comes back. Not which tier, not which command. What outcome do you want?

Finance says the daily revenue figure in the dashboard disagrees with the ledger. We need the number to be reproducible, and we need to know why the two disagree.

Then five objective questions, answered in under a minute.

mkdir -p design/daily-revenue
python3 tools/sbe_intake.py design/daily-revenue

Does it cross a service, system, or team boundary? (y/n) y
Is it reversible in under an hour? (y/n) n
Does it touch money, partner data, personal data, or production state? (y/n) y
How many downstream consumers break if it is wrong? (none/some/many) many

tier T3 (artifacts required: 01, 02, 03, 04, 05, 06, 07)

Nobody negotiated that. It touches money, so the first match is T3 and all seven design artifacts are owed. Marc did not have to know what a tier was, and Tom did not get to argue it down because the sprint is tight.

The person in a hurry does not get to decide how much rigour applies.

An override exists, and it is deliberately awkward: three fields have to agree and a written reason of at least three words has to sit beside them. A half-declared override fails and names the fields that disagree. Whether the reason is a good one is human review, and the tool says so rather than pretending to judge it.

The tier rule is first-match-wins over five booleans, computed by sbe_intake.py and re-derived from the diff by sbe impact, which reports when the declared tier and the change disagree.

Tuesday

The grain, written in words, before anybody writes SQL

Priya writes the data model artifact. It takes forty minutes and it is the highest-value forty minutes of the sprint.

fct_daily_revenue is one row per order date, product line and currency.

The system of record for order amounts is the billing system, not the CRM.

Order to order line is one-to-many, mandatory.

That third line is where the eleven per cent went. Writing the cardinality down forces the question that a query never asks: if one order has three lines, what happens to the amount when the two are joined?

python3 tools/sbe_design.py design/daily-revenue

datamodel  PASS  6 entities, each with a system of record;
                 5 relationship line(s) read, each carrying cardinality
diagrams   FAIL  diagram element(s) trace to no declared entity: BillingLedger

The diagrams check just caught something real. Priya's context diagram names BillingLedger, and no artifact declares it. Either it is an entity with an owner, or it is a component, and until somebody says which, two artifacts already disagree on day two.

That is the whole point of design-phase checking. The disagreement is one line of text on Tuesday. It is a reconciliation project in November.

Five design checks run over a dossier directory: artifacts, decision record, data model, diagrams, placeholder. All five are gate severity and block under --strict. A tier requiring no artifacts reports NO-DATA rather than PASS.

Thursday

Prove the number, or label it

The model is fixed. The figure moves. Now somebody has to be able to say why the new one is right, and "we re-ran it" is what failed in March.

The numbers gate wants three things: a pinned snapshot so the re-run reads the same bytes, a second derivation whose text genuinely differs, and zero drift between the two real numbers.

-- derivation A, the model, pinned to a Delta version
SELECT order_date, SUM(net_amount) FROM analytics.fct_daily_revenue
VERSION AS OF 412 GROUP BY order_date;

-- derivation B, from the source, deliberately a different path
SELECT o.order_date, SUM(l.quantity * l.unit_price - l.discount)
FROM raw.orders o JOIN raw.order_lines l ON l.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY o.order_date;

Derivation B walks a different path on purpose. That is the part a person has to get right, because the gate cannot check it.

The limit, stated where it bites

The gate compares the two derivations as text and requires them to differ by more than case, whitespace and comments. It does not read which tables they touch. Renaming an alias passes. Two queries over the same broken view agree perfectly and pass.

That is a floor, not a proof of independence, and it is printed on every run. A team that believes otherwise will trust it at exactly the wrong moment.

Marc, meanwhile, is doing the part that has no gate at all. He writes the acceptance criteria against what Finance actually asked for, and the QA traceability review maps each one to a test that exists.

sbe review-route --base main
data-reviewer      models/, *.sql changed
migration-reviewer migrations/ changed
at most two specialists; zero is a legal result

No model chose those two. The router read the diff.

Friday

Merge, or do not

Adaeze opens the pull request. CI runs the same checks the team has been running locally all week, this time under --strict.

Blockers first. Then, plainly, what was never looked at.

The approval line is NO-DATA rather than PASS because the team has not wired signing keys into CI yet. It does not block, it is not hidden, and it will still be there next Friday until somebody does something about it.

Staff engineer, principal engineer or hands-on tech lead

Stop being the human checksum for every AI-generated change

Your judgement is the scarcest thing on the team, and it is currently being spent re-reading work rather than shaping it.

The pressure

What changes on Monday

Nothing philosophical. Four concrete differences, all visible in the repository.

  • The tier is computed before anyone opens an editor. Five intake questions, first match wins, T0 to T3. A T0 change owes nothing and does not arrive on your desk carrying a dossier. A T3 change that has cleared the design check arrives with its decisions already written down, because the check FAILs otherwise.
  • The decision you have explained three times becomes a file. 03-adr.md carries the decision, two genuinely distinct rejected alternatives, and the flip condition that would reopen it. The design check FAILs an ADR missing any of those.
  • Reviewer selection stops being you by default. sbe review-route reads the diff and names at most two specialist reviewers, deterministically, with no model choosing. Zero is a legal result rather than a claim that a review happened. It names specialist roles and adds nobody to a pull request, so who is actually asked stays your team's rule.
  • Your standard becomes a registered check with a receipt, or it stays a comment. The check registry names the executable, its exact argument vector, its working directory, the files it is evidence for, and the runner files whose bytes are the check.

Underneath it sits a defect worth understanding, because it is the reason the evidence layer is shaped the way it is.

A receipt used to prove that a process started, was timed, and exited. So true minted a clean, sealed, commit-bound receipt.

The command ran. That was provable.

The command was the check this change owed. That was not.

How do you run three workers at once without a merge nobody can unpick?

Parallelism is where small teams lose their nerve, and they are right to. Two agents editing the same file at the same time produces a merge you cannot reason about, and the cost lands on the person who has to unpick it.

The practical effect on a small team is that scope stops being a conversation. A brief names the paths in scope, the paths that must not be touched, the dependencies, the acceptance criteria, the commands that count as verification, the constraints already known, and the conditions on which to stop rather than improvise. Two runs of the same brief produce byte-identical JSON, and a brief that would exceed its size limit is refused rather than truncated, because a silently shortened brief is a scope change nobody agreed to.

From a business outcome to a merge decision, and back again

Who reviews this change, and why it is not a matter of opinion

No model decides which specialist reviews the change. The diff does.

The order is fixed and unarguable: migration, then security, then data, then backend, then infrastructure, then QA, then evidence. At most two reviewers are ever selected, and how many slots exist depends only on the tier. The router reads the diff, the intake file when one is discoverable, and the evidence store when one exists, and nothing else: no clock, no random source, no model call. Run it twice on the same two commits and you get the same answer, which is the property that lets a person argue with it.

Ownership moves when somebody accepts it, not when somebody sends it

The record derives what it can and refuses to invent the rest. Done, in flight and not started come from the plan compared against the task registry. Active tasks carry their worktree and base commit. Every worktree is checked for uncommitted work, and every evidence item is tagged current, stale or unavailable. Open questions, decisions and required access are written empty on purpose, because the tool cannot know them and a plausible guess in that field is worse than a blank one.

Tab four

Why trust it

Everything claimed so far is worth exactly what the mechanics underneath it are worth. This part is the mechanics, in the order a sceptical engineer asks for them: what a verdict means, what happens before code, what a receipt is, who is allowed to write, what actually stops a merge, and every place the system will not help you.

What does a green build actually prove?

Most tooling has two answers: it passed, or it failed. That is one answer short, and the missing one is where wrong results live.

A check can pass. A check can fail. And a check can find that there was nothing there to read at all, which is a completely different fact and deserves its own word.

Absent evidence is NO-DATA. NO-DATA is never a pass.

Three of the situations below contain no evidence whatsoever. A two-state system has only one place to put them, and it is the good one.

This is not a convention anybody has to police. A check is refused registration at construction if it declares PASS as its empty state, so the rule cannot be forgotten by a check written next year. A meta-test walks the registries themselves rather than a hand-typed list, so a new check is covered the day it is registered.

The same discovery rule generates the check register printed later in this part, which is why that table and the honesty test can never drift apart: they enumerate the same objects.

The fine print, printed here rather than buried

NO-DATA is never a pass as a verdict, and it is not a block as an exit code. Under --strict, a FAIL exits nonzero and a NO-DATA prints and exits zero. So a change that declares nothing at all can produce several NO-DATA lines and a green build.

That is deliberate, and it is also the sharpest edge in the product. It means a clean run is not a safety claim. It means the question to ask of a green build is not "did anything fail", it is "how many of these lines say NO-DATA, and why". A team that reads a repeated NO-DATA as noise has turned a control into decoration.

Why design before code, rather than better review?

The decisions that cost the most are taken before there is a diff to review. The grain of a table. The contract two other teams already depend on. Which of five stores is the system of record. By the time a reviewer opens a pull request, all of those are settled, and the review is an argument about spelling.

So the order is fixed. A change is scored first, by five objective questions with a first-match-wins ladder, and the tier decides how much writing it owes. Most changes come out at the bottom tier and owe nothing at all, which is the only reason the top tier can afford to owe a great deal.

What the design checks then read is shape, never content, and this is where the mechanism stops and your judgment starts. The data-model check fails an entity with no named system of record and a relationship with no stated cardinality. The decision record must carry two distinct rejected alternatives and a condition that would flip it. A diagram node that traces back to no declared entity fails. A file still carrying the shipped template fails. That is the shape of it, and shape is all of it.

None of it can tell a correct answer from a confident wrong one. Naming the wrong system of record passes every check that ships. A one-sentence purpose brief passes. A complete dossier written after the code passes identically to one written before it.

The value is the argument the data model forces out of a room. The file is only the receipt for having had it.

Why a receipt beats a green stage

A green stage is a name and a conclusion. Nothing in it records what ran, how long it took, or which files it was looking at. That is why a skipped job and a four-minute job look the same from the merge rule's side of the wall.

A receipt is minted by the runner that executed the command, never typed by the thing making the claim. It seals the command with its arguments redacted, the exit code, the duration, the base and head commit, and a content digest of every path the check declared it covers.

What a receipt lets you askWhat a green stage can answer
Which exact command produced this?Nothing. A name is not a command.
Did it take any time at all?Nothing. A zero-second success is still a success.
Which commit was it looking at?The head of a branch that has since moved.
Have the files it covered changed since?Nothing. Digests were never taken.
Was there a receipt at all?Nothing, and the absence looks like success.

That last row is the ran gate, and it is the smallest idea in the product: a check that exited zero having taken no time did not run. Registration matters for the same reason. A check has an entry naming the command it binds, so a receipt points at something declared in advance, and editing that entry invalidates every receipt minted before the edit.

Now the ceiling, because a receipt proves less than the word suggests. The highest trust level a continuous integration run reaches is CI-claimed, which the module's own text defines as CI-shaped metadata that nothing verified. A laptop exporting the same run identifier on a clean tree earns the same label. The seal is a checksum, not a signature, and there is no protected evidence level in this release. Receipts are also strictly commit-bound: one minted before a rebase fails at the merge commit, and a covered file rewritten afterwards invalidates it even when the bytes are identical, which is why a receipt is written outside the tree it covers and regenerated by the pipeline on the final head.

The trust level is printed on every receipt rather than left to be inferred, and a run carrying no pipeline run identifier is labelled local-advisory. What the label records is what the environment claimed, not where the run happened, which is exactly why the level above it is called CI-claimed rather than CI-verified.

What actually stops a merge

Four failure classes get a hard gate, chosen because each of them fails quietly rather than loudly.

Read the last column as the design decision it is. The default is NO-DATA, because absence genuinely might mean "there was no migration in this change". The approval gate is the one place a broken claim is told apart from an absent one: declaring an approval and putting no identity behind it is a claim that failed, so it reads FAIL, while a change that declared nothing reads NO-DATA like everything else. Self-approval fails at both ends.

Two things follow from that, and both of them cut against the product. A change that quietly needed an approval and never declared one is a green build, because there was nothing to read. And the approval gate is only ever real where the reviewers' public keys are imported on the machine running it: an agent without them reports an unverifiable signature, so every approval on that host reads NO-DATA no matter how carefully it was signed. A file naming code owners does not block anything by itself. It nominates reviewers. Something behind it, branch protection or a branch ruleset, has to require their review before the nomination has teeth. A repository with the file and neither of those has a suggestion, not a control.

Advisory first, enforcing later is the right rollout, and it is not universally available. On the host that offers branch rulesets, the advisory evaluate mode is gated at organisation and enterprise level, so a smaller plan gets on or off rather than a soft first fortnight. If you are on one of those plans, get the same effect by wrapping the job in your own pipeline's soft-failure mechanism for the first two weeks, then unwrap one stage at a time.

What stays human, and what you have to supply

Three things are worth being blunt about before anybody signs anything.

It never gets apply rights of its own. Nothing in it merges, approves, deploys or publishes, no role it ships is given a merge, push or deploy right, and it never learns that a human did any of those things. It drafts the command and its rollback, and a human runs it. Be equally clear about what that is not: it is not a fence between an agent and your production. Every role it ships holds a shell, so the thing that actually stops a deploy is the credentials on the machine and the rights on your host, and those stay your job. A manual approval step in your pipeline and a signed approval trailer are also two different approvals, and treating them as one is the most dangerous sentence available in this area.

It stops at the deploy. No monitoring, no alerting, no error budget, no rollback trigger. No receipt describes production state, so the question your support team actually asks, is it live and is it fixed, has no answer here.

It sees only what passes through a diff, a JSON file or a markdown file. A field retyped and saved in an administration console, a campaign built in a marketing tool, a voucher rule, a content type published from a headless store: none of those produce a diff, so none of them gets a design phase, a gate, a linter or a receipt, and nothing notices the omission. On the one estate this was mapped against, a large share of what the commercial and support side logged as a bug turned out to be product data or content rather than code. That is one team's experience rather than a measured rule, and the useful move is to check the same thing on your own tickets before assuming the gap is small. Say it on day one either way, not after the first miss.

What the team supplies is the other half of the system, and it is not a short list: the pipeline job, the merge rule, the branch protection, the signing keys on the machine that verifies approvals, the entry describing each registered check, and the export that makes a schema visible to git in the first place when it lives in a tool rather than in a file.

And then the judgment, which no part of this touches. Whether the system of record you named is the right one. Whether the alternative you rejected was the one worth rejecting. Whether the second derivation is genuinely independent or merely differently worded. Whether the check somebody registered is a good check. The product's position is that it makes those questions visible, arguable and dated, and answers none of them.

Tab five

Start small

You are not buying a process. You are testing one claim, on one change, this week: that your senior people can stop being the standing verification layer for everything the machine writes. Here is the smallest honest way to find out, what it costs, how to tell it is working, how to tell it is not, and how to take it back out.

Nothing in the first fortnight changes how anything merges. It is files in your own repository, read by people who already work there. The first thing that could stop a merge arrives in week eight, and it is your own platform that does the stopping.

What should we point it at first?

Pick the change you are already dreading. Not a toy, and not the riskiest thing in the backlog either: the ordinary change that you know, from experience, is going to cost somebody senior an afternoon of checking. Six seats, six versions of that same instruction.

Seat one

The staff engineer: the change you already know will land on your desk

Take the next change you are certain you will be asked to review, and run the intake with its author in the room before they write anything. You are not reviewing earlier. You are moving the argument you were going to have in a pull request to the hour where it is cheap, and putting it in a file that the next person can read. When the diff arrives, the reviewer routing picks specialists from the diff itself rather than from whoever is free, and every routed reviewer is read-only by construction.

Seat two

The backend engineer: the contract change whose consumers nobody has enumerated

Be clear about what this does and does not do. It does not go and find your consumers, and it does not stop the change. What it does is make the change harder to treat as a small one: the tier is computed from five answers rather than chosen, and reading the diff afterwards proposes a floor. That floor is a proposal. The tool moves nobody's declared tier in either direction, so a tier declared below the floor comes back as a disagreement somebody has to record by name, against a named commit. The list of who breaks is written by a human, before the change, into the process and data-model artifacts, and the reviewer who reads it can name what is missing. The improvement is that the list exists and is dated, not that a machine produced it.

Seat three

The data engineer: the number Finance already questioned once

Take the figure somebody has already challenged and that survived the challenge because you re-ran the model and got the same answer. Register it: a second derivation whose text genuinely differs from the first, re-run to zero drift against a pinned snapshot. Know the height of the floor before you stand on it. The gate checks that the two derivations differ by more than case, whitespace and comments, and that the two figures recorded from the re-run agree. It executes no query and cannot read which tables each derivation touches, so two reads of the same broken view still agree and still pass. It is a floor worth having and a ceiling worth printing on the wall.

Seat four

The QA engineer: the release where the suite is green and you still do not trust it

Wrap one real suite in the evidence runner, so the claim that it ran carries a receipt naming the command, its exit code, its duration and the paths it covered, minted by the runner rather than typed by anybody. Then look at what the receipt says about the runs you were previously calling green. A check that took no time did not run. A registered check that produced nothing comes back NO-DATA, and NO-DATA is never a pass.

Seat five

The technical business analyst: the requirement that has changed meaning three times

Write the decision record for it: the criteria, two genuinely distinct rejected alternatives, and the condition that would flip the decision back. The check reads the shape, never the judgement. It refuses a file still carrying the shipped template and refuses a record with no flip condition, and a thin but complete answer passes. The value this week is the argument the flip condition forces, and the fact that in three months the argument is still on disk rather than in somebody's memory of a call.

Seat six

The platform engineer: the migration whose rollback has never actually been run

Rehearse it forward and backward against a restored copy, and record the row counts on both sides. The gate reads that receipt; it does not produce it, and nothing resolves the rehearsal id against your job system, so that field is a pointer for a human to follow. A receipt with no row counts is NO-DATA rather than a pass, which is the entire point: the gate says what it compared instead of asserting a comparison it never made.

Every mechanism named above ships with tests and runs in this project's own CI. What none of them do: read your purpose or process artifacts for content, detect that a change needed an approval nobody declared, enumerate consumers, or block anything on their own.

The one that surprises people in week nine

A receipt is bound to two things on purpose: the head commit it was minted against, and the exact bytes of every file it says it covers. Both have to still hold when somebody checks it. So if your repository rewrites history on merge, a receipt minted on the branch tip stops matching at the merge commit, and any later commit at all moves the head out from under it. That is strict by design, and it means regenerating receipts on the post-merge head belongs in your pipeline as a designed step rather than as something you work out during an incident.
