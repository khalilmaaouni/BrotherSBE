Act one

The pain

Six short sections. What is going wrong on teams shipping backend and data work with AI, what stops it immediately, what stops it coming back, and the one incident that started all of this.

Your team did not agree to become the reviewer of record for a machine

The more engineering you hand to Claude, the more of your week goes to deciding whether what came back is true.

Your team is small. Two engineers, maybe five, maybe eight. Between you there is a backend, some infrastructure, a warehouse, a report the business actually looks at, and a test suite somebody has to believe in. Claude now writes a great deal of that, and it writes it fast. That is the good news, and it is genuinely good.

Here is the part nobody warns you about. More AI-written engineering creates more verification, and verification is the expensive kind of work.

What your team ends up doingHow often it shows up
Re-deriving a number by hand to see whether the first one was rightEvery figure that reaches a decision
Reading a migration to work out whether the rollback would surviveEvery schema change
Reconstructing why a table has the grain it hasEvery time the model is questioned
Working out who actually consumes an endpoint before changing itEvery contract change
Deciding whether a test proves the thing it claims to proveEvery review
Re-running the check yourself because you do not trust the reportEvery time done is asserted
Explaining to Finance where a figure came fromEvery month end

Eventually your senior engineer is the design review, the migration gate and the reconciliation, for a machine that never gets tired.

The painkiller: stop taking the agent's word for it

Four things stop hurting in the first week.

A small team. More output. Less taking it on faith.

The design checks, the four hard gates and the silent-failure linter each ship with named tests and run in CI on Linux and Windows. The tier computation is re-derived from the diff and compared against what was declared. Act five prints the whole register with the gap beside each entry.

The medicine: stop the same class of wrong from becoming normal

Catching this week's bad migration is worth something. It is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is that four failures keep coming back on backend and data teams, every one of them silent, and each is closed by a different phase of the same loop.

The number that agreed with itself

A daily revenue figure had been in the board pack for five months. Finance queried it once, in March, and an engineer checked it by re-running the model. The model returned the same number. Everyone moved on.

In August somebody joined orders to order lines to answer a different question, and the total came out lower. Not by a rounding error. By eleven per cent.

The model had been fanning out on a join since the week it was written. Re-running it had confirmed nothing, because the check and the thing being checked were the same query.

Nobody was careless. The engineer who checked it did exactly what checking looks like. The failure was structural: a second derivation that walks the same path is not a second derivation, and nothing in the pipeline knew the difference.

A check that reads the same path as the work is not a check. It is the work, run twice.

This is what the numbers gate exists for, and it is also the clearest example of an honest limit. The gate demands a second derivation whose text genuinely differs from the first, re-run to zero drift against a pinned snapshot. It cannot read which tables each derivation touches. Two queries over the same broken view still agree, and the gate still passes them.

That floor is worth having and it is worth knowing the height of. A team that believes the gate proves independence will trust it at exactly the wrong moment, which is why the limit is printed on every run rather than kept in a footnote.

Designs, checks, proves

Three words on the cover, and each is a different mechanism.

PillarWhat it means concretelyWhat enforces it
DesignsSix phases run in order before code: purpose, process, architecture, data, expression, verification. The output is a dossier of at most eight files in one directory.Five design checks, blocking in CI: artifacts, decision record, data model, diagrams, and no file left as the shipped template.
ChecksFour failure classes get a structural gate, chosen because each fails silently: numbers, migration, approval, and whether a check actually ran.sbe_gate.py, advisory in a session, blocking under --strict in CI.
ProvesA claim is backed by a receipt naming what ran, its exit code and its duration, or it is labelled UNVERIFIED next to the item.The evidence store and the ran gate. A check that took no time did not run.

Underneath all three sits one rule that is easier to state than to find elsewhere.

Absent evidence is NO-DATA. NO-DATA is never a pass.

Three verdicts, not two. Something passed. Something failed. And something was never there to read, which is reported by name, and which never decides an exit code in either direction. Most tooling has two states and quietly sorts "did not run" into the good one. That collapse is where wrong results live.

Enforced at construction rather than by convention: a check is refused registration if it declares PASS as its empty state, and a meta-test enumerates the registries rather than a written list, so a check added next year is covered on the day it is registered.

What it is, and three things it is not

BrotherSBE is a Claude Code plugin that behaves like a senior backend and data engineering colleague. You describe the outcome in plain language. It decides the method, works in the order the engineering actually runs, and refuses to call anything finished until a check has run and left a receipt.

It is not a prompt pack. A skill pack changes how the model writes. This changes what has to exist on disk before a merge is allowed. A rule in a prompt is not a control; a control is a check that runs.

It is not a linter. A linter reads the diff, which means it arrives after the grain was chosen and the boundary was drawn. There is a linter inside this, hunting the patterns that swallow errors, but it is one of the smaller parts.

It is not a replacement for your reviewers. It routes reviewers deterministically from the diff and gives each a narrow brief, and every one of them is read-only by construction. It does not approve anything. Nothing here detects that a change needed a human approval in the first place.

It is the domain specialist sibling of BrotherMode, the general orchestrator. They share a spine and diverge on scope. BrotherSBE is standalone: clone it and it works with nothing else installed.

Act two

One team, one sprint

Four people, five days. Finance says the daily revenue figure disagrees with the ledger. Fixing it turns out to mean a data model, a schema migration, a service contract and a test suite nobody trusts. Every command appears where the sprint needs it, not before.

The team: Priya on the warehouse, Tom on the service, Adaeze on infrastructure and release, and Marc, the Technical BA who owns what the business actually asked for. Nobody has a spare week.

Monday

The work is sized before it is scoped

Marc opens the session and says what happened, in the words Finance used.

/brothersbe:start

One question comes back. Not which tier, not which command. What outcome do you want?

Finance says the daily revenue figure in the dashboard disagrees with the ledger. We need the number to be reproducible, and we need to know why the two disagree.

Then five objective questions, answered in under a minute.

mkdir -p design/daily-revenue
python3 tools/sbe_intake.py design/daily-revenue

Does this change a data model, an API contract, or a file interface others depend on? (y/n) y
Does it cross a service, system, or team boundary? (y/n) y
Is it reversible in under an hour? (y/n) n
Does it touch money, partner data, personal data, or production state? (y/n) y
How many downstream consumers break if it is wrong? (none/some/many) many

tier T3 (artifacts required: 01, 02, 03, 04, 05, 06, 07)

Nobody negotiated that. It touches money, so the first match is T3 and all seven design artifacts are owed. Marc did not have to know what a tier was, and Tom did not get to argue it down because the sprint is tight.

The person in a hurry does not get to decide how much rigour applies.

An override exists, and it is deliberately awkward: three fields have to agree and a written reason of at least three words has to sit beside them. A half-declared override fails and names the fields that disagree. Whether the reason is a good one is human review, and the tool says so rather than pretending to judge it.

The tier rule is first-match-wins over five booleans, computed by sbe_intake.py and re-derived from the diff by sbe impact, which reports when the declared tier and the change disagree.

Tuesday

The grain, written in words, before anybody writes SQL

Priya writes the data model artifact. It takes forty minutes and it is the highest-value forty minutes of the sprint.

fct_daily_revenue is one row per order date, product line and currency.

The system of record for order amounts is the billing system, not the CRM.

Order to order line is one-to-many, mandatory.

That third line is where the eleven per cent went. Writing the cardinality down forces the question that a query never asks: if one order has three lines, what happens to the amount when the two are joined?

python3 tools/sbe_design.py design/daily-revenue

datamodel  PASS  6 entities, each with a system of record;
                 5 relationship line(s) read, each carrying cardinality
diagrams   FAIL  diagram element(s) trace to no declared entity: BillingLedger

The diagrams check just caught something real. Priya's context diagram names BillingLedger, and no artifact declares it. Either it is an entity with an owner, or it is a component, and until somebody says which, two artifacts already disagree on day two.

That is the whole point of design-phase checking. The disagreement is one line of text on Tuesday. It is a reconciliation project in November.

Five design checks run over a dossier directory: artifacts, decision record, data model, diagrams, placeholder. All five are gate severity and block under --strict. A tier requiring no artifacts reports NO-DATA rather than PASS.

Wednesday, morning

The rollback nobody had run

The fix needs a schema change: the aggregate gains a currency column and the old one is retired. Adaeze writes the migration. Claude writes it quickly and correctly, and says it is done.

It is not done, and this is where the gate does something a reviewer usually cannot.

Adaeze restores a copy, runs both legs, records the receipt through the evidence wrapper, and the gate passes on evidence rather than on reading.

sbe evidence run --check migration-rehearsal -- \
  scripts/rehearse_migration.sh --restore nightly-2026-08-05

sbe gate migration design/daily-revenue
migration  PASS  forward and reverse both recorded against restore
                 nightly-2026-08-05; rehearsal id RH-4471; 8,412,905 rows
                 before and after [severity: gate]

The reverse leg is not documentation. It is a thing that has either run or not.

Wednesday, afternoon

Two writers, one file

Tom is changing the service that serves the figure. Priya is changing the model underneath it. Both need to touch the same transformation file, and both have an agent working.

On a small team this is the failure that costs an afternoon and nobody writes a post-mortem about it, because it is embarrassing rather than interesting.

sbe task open DR-12 --agent priya --paths models/revenue.sql
sbe fences
DR-12  priya  models/revenue.sql  open
Thursday

Prove the number, or label it

The model is fixed. The figure moves. Now somebody has to be able to say why the new one is right, and "we re-ran it" is what failed in March.

The numbers gate wants three things: a pinned snapshot so the re-run reads the same bytes, a second derivation whose text genuinely differs, and zero drift between the two real numbers.

-- derivation A, the model, pinned to a Delta version
SELECT order_date, SUM(net_amount) FROM analytics.fct_daily_revenue
VERSION AS OF 412 GROUP BY order_date;

-- derivation B, from the source, deliberately a different path
SELECT o.order_date, SUM(l.quantity * l.unit_price - l.discount)
FROM raw.orders o JOIN raw.order_lines l ON l.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY o.order_date;

Derivation B walks a different path on purpose. That is the part a person has to get right, because the gate cannot check it.

The limit, stated where it bites

The gate compares the two derivations as text and requires them to differ by more than case, whitespace and comments. It does not read which tables they touch. Renaming an alias passes. Two queries over the same broken view agree perfectly and pass.

That is a floor, not a proof of independence, and it is printed on every run. A team that believes otherwise will trust it at exactly the wrong moment.

Marc, meanwhile, is doing the part that has no gate at all. He writes the acceptance criteria against what Finance actually asked for, and the QA traceability review maps each one to a test that exists.

sbe review-route --base main
data-reviewer      models/, *.sql changed
migration-reviewer migrations/ changed
at most two specialists; zero is a legal result

No model chose those two. The router read the diff.

Friday

Merge, or do not

Adaeze opens the pull request. CI runs the same checks the team has been running locally all week, this time under --strict.

sbe status

BROKEN CLAIMS:   clean
MERGE BLOCKERS:  clean
MISSING EVIDENCE: NO-DATA. dossier daily-revenue: no receipt declares
                 an approval, and nothing here detects that one was needed
NEXT ACTION: nothing blocking here that this tool can see.

Read that last block carefully, because it is the register this whole product is written in. Nothing is blocking. That is not the same claim as everything having been inspected, and the output says which is which rather than letting the reader assume.

Blockers first. Then, plainly, what was never looked at.

The approval line is NO-DATA rather than PASS because the team has not wired signing keys into CI yet. It does not block, it is not hidden, and it will still be there next Friday until somebody does something about it.

Act three

Five seats at the table

On a small team one person often holds two of these. Find the seat you actually sit in: the pain in your own words, the gate that fires for you, and what changes on Monday.

Seat one

Backend engineer

Your expensive mistakes are a boundary drawn in the wrong place and a contract changed without knowing who consumed it.

The pain. Claude will happily change an endpoint's response shape. It cannot know that a partner batch job parses that response at 2am, because nobody wrote that down anywhere it could read. You find out from the partner.

What fires for you. The intake asks whether this changes a contract others depend on and how many consumers break if it is wrong. Answer yes and many and you are at T2, owing six artifacts, one of which is a decision record with two genuinely distinct rejected alternatives and a flip condition.

The part that pays. The flip condition, which is the field teams skip.

Revisit toward a split if a second team comes to need independent deployment of the pricing path, or if the consistency requirement moves from eventual to strict for order totals.

Neither is true today: one team deploys this, and Finance reconciles daily rather than continuously.

Six months later that paragraph is the difference between revisiting a decision and re-litigating it from nothing.

On the exception path. The process artifact asks for it by name, and on service work the exception path is the document. "On a timeout the order stays in pending_payment and a reconciliation job resolves it within fifteen minutes, never retried inline, because the provider is not idempotent on this endpoint without an idempotency key and we do not send one today" is the sentence that prevents a double charge. It exists because a template demanded it.

Your reviewer. backend-reviewer, read-only, covering contract compatibility, idempotency, concurrency, transaction boundaries, error paths, retries, observability and performance. Touch auth, money, partner APIs, file upload, secrets or personal data and security-reviewer joins it automatically.

Reviewer selection is deterministic: sbe review-route reads the diff, no model chooses, at most two specialists, and zero is a legal result rather than a claim of a clean review.

Seat three

Infrastructure engineer

You are the one who has to believe the rollback, and you are the one holding the pager when it turns out nobody tested it.

The pain. A migration that reads correct is not a migration that has been run backwards on real data. Expand and contract compressed into one release is the single most common way a rollback becomes impossible, and it looks completely fine in review.

The sequence that survives.

  1. Expand. Add the new column nullable, deploy, write to both.
  2. Backfill, with the reconciliation registered as a check and run through the evidence wrapper.
  3. Read from the new column, deploy.
  4. Contract. Drop the old column, in a later release, only once the previous one is no longer deployable.

Step four in the same release as step one is the failure. migration-reviewer covers exactly this: forward and reverse evidence, expand and contract compatibility, lock duration, mixed-schema deployment and rollback time.

Where the blast radius rule sits with you. BrotherSBE never gets apply rights on production state. It drafts the exact command with its rollback and hands it to a human. Credentials are never typed, stored or logged.

Say this out loud before you adopt it

That rule is a stated discipline plus whatever access control your estate already has. Nothing in this plugin can revoke a credential your shell already holds. If your real requirement is that an agent physically cannot deploy, that is your CI permissions and your cloud IAM, not a line in a skill file.

What you must supply yourself. No CODEOWNERS and no branch protection ships here. The CI workflow guards nothing until you copy it into your own repository, and --strict moves only by a human editing that workflow, visible in the diff. That last property is deliberate: a session instruction can never waive a hard gate.

Seat four

QA engineer

Your problem is not writing tests. It is that a green suite and a correct system are two different claims, and only one of them is being made.

The pain. An agent writes tests alongside the code, and they pass. Of course they pass: they were written from the same understanding that produced the code. A test that encodes the same misunderstanding as the implementation is worse than no test, because it converts an open question into a settled one.

What fires for you. The ran gate, which is blunter than it sounds and more useful than it sounds. Each recorded check must name what ran, exit zero, and take a nonzero duration. A check that took no time did not run.

Underneath it sits a defect worth understanding, because it is the reason the evidence layer is shaped the way it is.

A receipt used to prove that a process started, was timed, and exited. So -- true minted a clean, sealed, commit-bound receipt.

The command ran. That was provable.

The command was the check this change owed. That was not.

Now the check registry names the executable, its exact argument vector, its working directory, the files it is evidence for, and the runner files whose bytes are the check. sbe evidence run --check <id> resolves all of that from the registry, and there is no argument on that path that changes what runs.

Your reviewer. qa-reviewer maps requirements and acceptance criteria to executable tests, finds missing negative and non-functional coverage, and validates that the test evidence says what it is claimed to say. On interface and service work the missing coverage is almost always the failure path: the network error, the declined card, the expired session.

The rule that changes your review verdicts. A verdict counts only when it names the falsification actually executed: the command re-run, the defect reproduced, the number re-derived. Reasoning alone is NO-DATA, not a finding.

That rule is graded at the weekly review and nothing parses a verdict, so it is a discipline rather than a control. It is listed among the honest limits for exactly that reason.

Seat five

Technical BA

You are the only person in the room whose output has no gate at all, and that is precisely why the design phase was built.

The pain. The business asks for something in its own words. By the time it reaches a schema, three translations have happened and nobody recorded any of them. When the delivered thing is wrong, the argument is about what was asked for, and there is no artifact to settle it.

Where you own the document. The purpose brief is yours, and it has four parts that each stop a specific argument.

SectionThe argument it prevents
Problem, stated with no solution in itBuilding the first idea somebody had instead of the thing that is broken
Users, and what they do today insteadSolving for a user who does not exist
Success criteria, observable"It feels faster" as an acceptance criterion
Non-goals, explicitThe scope creep that turns a fix into a quarter of work
What breaks if this is wrongDiscovering the blast radius during the incident

The non-goals section earns its place fastest. "This does not change how revenue is recognised" is one line, and it is the line that stops a reporting fix becoming an accounting project.

Observable, concretely. "A user can complete checkout without leaving the page, and a failed payment leaves no orphaned order" is checkable by somebody who was not in the room. "The checkout feels faster" is not, and no gate downstream can rescue it.

What you get back. The verification artifact turns your acceptance criteria into a table of one row per claim, the check that proves it, and when it runs. A claim with no check is listed as having none, rather than omitted, which is the difference between a plan and a wish.

When it earns its overhead

An honest test, because a control your team switches off protects nothing.

Adopt it whenDo not bother when

On a T0 change it costs five questions and owes nothing. That is the common case, and it is meant to be.

On a T2 or T3 change the cost is real: six or seven artifacts, each a piece of thinking somebody has to do. The argument is not that this is free. It is that you were going to pay for those decisions anyway, and design time is cheaper than incident time.

If your changes are almost never T2 or T3, you probably do not need this. That is a legitimate answer and nothing here is going to argue you out of it.

What it does not do

Stated here rather than discovered in month two.

  • It does not deploy. No apply rights on production state, ever. It drafts the exact command and its rollback for a human to run.
  • It does not notice that a change needed an approval. The gate verifies an approval that was declared. A money-path change that declares nothing sails past it. What you need there is CODEOWNERS and branch protection, and neither ships here.
  • It does not prove two derivations are independent. It proves their text differs. That is a floor.
  • It does not resolve a rehearsal identifier against your job system. A human follows that pointer.
  • It does not judge whether a stated reason is a good one. It checks that a reviewable reason exists and is not a refused token.
  • It does not measure the spine. "Design before verification" and "install the check before writing the work" are disciplines. No tool computes whether you followed them.
  • It has no opinion on visual design, typography or accessibility. Different toolchain, no overlap.
  • It does not replace your judgement about your own domain. The datamodel check enforces that every entity names an owner. It cannot tell you that you named the wrong one.

A control whose limits are hidden is a control you will over-trust at exactly the wrong moment.

Every reviewer is read-only, holding Read, Grep, Glob, Bash and nothing else. Exactly one role in that table holds Edit and Write, and it is the implementation worker, which writes code and reviews nothing.

That separation is structural rather than instructional, and it is the cheapest control in the system. A reviewer that can edit will fix what it finds, and a finding fixed in silence is a finding nobody recorded.

The four that matter most to a small team

Nothing detects that a change needed an approval. The gate verifies an approval that was declared. What you actually need is CODEOWNERS and branch protection on your own repository.

Two derivations differing in text is a floor, not a proof of independence. Two queries over the same broken view agree perfectly.

A rehearsal identifier is a pointer, not a resolution. Nothing calls your job system to confirm the rehearsal happened.

This repository's own merged pull requests carry no independent review. Applied to its own history, the approval law reports NO-DATA, not PASS. That is disclosed rather than hidden, and it is the honest answer to whether the project practises what it sells on that one axis: not yet, and it says so.

The measurement caveat. Every threshold this project ships, and every baseline in its rubric, was measured on one estate. One. They are starting points rather than constants, and the honest instruction is to re-measure them on yours.

Stating the limits is what makes the rest of the claims worth anything.

Your first sprint

One repository. One lane of work. Two weeks advisory, then strict. Do not roll this across an estate before one team has an opinion, because a control that arrives everywhere at once gets switched off everywhere at once the first time it is inconvenient.

WhenWhat you doWhat you should see
Week 0sbe adopt ., which is a dry run and changes nothing. Read it. Then --apply.A list of what would be installed, and nothing installed until you say so.
Week 1Copy the CI workflow in, without --strict. Run the sprint normally.Verdicts printed, nothing blocked. This week exists to find what is noisy on your codebase.
Week 2Agree two sentences out loud, as a team, before turning it on."A FAIL blocks, a NO-DATA does not." And: "Nobody edits the workflow to make red go away."
Week 3Turn on --strict.One uncomfortable pull request. That is the system working.
Month 3Read the weekly review honestly.Most changes T0. Repeated NO-DATA treated as a workflow problem rather than a tool problem. Flip conditions occasionally met.

That second sentence in week two is the entire control. It is why --strict moves only by a human editing the workflow file, where a reviewer sees it in the diff, and why a session instruction can never waive a hard gate.

Try it on the change you would be most embarrassed to get wrong.
