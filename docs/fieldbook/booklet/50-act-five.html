Every reviewer is read-only, holding Read, Grep, Glob, Bash and nothing else. Exactly one role in that table holds Edit and Write, and it is the implementation worker, which writes code and reviews nothing.

That separation is structural rather than instructional, and it is the cheapest control in the system. A reviewer that can edit will fix what it finds, and a finding fixed in silence is a finding nobody recorded.

The four that matter most to a small team

Nothing detects that a change needed an approval. The gate verifies an approval that was declared. What you actually need is CODEOWNERS and branch protection on your own repository.

Two derivations differing in text is a floor, not a proof of independence. Two queries over the same broken view agree perfectly.

A rehearsal identifier is a pointer, not a resolution. Nothing calls your job system to confirm the rehearsal happened.

This repository's own merged pull requests carry no independent review. Applied to its own history, the approval law reports NO-DATA, not PASS. That is disclosed rather than hidden, and it is the honest answer to whether the project practises what it sells on that one axis: not yet, and it says so.

The measurement caveat. Every threshold this project ships, and every baseline in its rubric, was measured on one estate. One. They are starting points rather than constants, and the honest instruction is to re-measure them on yours.

Stating the limits is what makes the rest of the claims worth anything.

Your first sprint

One repository. One lane of work. Two weeks advisory, then strict. Do not roll this across an estate before one team has an opinion, because a control that arrives everywhere at once gets switched off everywhere at once the first time it is inconvenient.

WhenWhat you doWhat you should see
Week 0sbe adopt ., which is a dry run and changes nothing. Read it. Then --apply.A list of what would be installed, and nothing installed until you say so.
Week 1Copy the CI workflow in, without --strict. Run the sprint normally.Verdicts printed, nothing blocked. This week exists to find what is noisy on your codebase.
Week 2Agree two sentences out loud, as a team, before turning it on."A FAIL blocks, a NO-DATA does not." And: "Nobody edits the workflow to make red go away."
Week 3Turn on --strict.One uncomfortable pull request. That is the system working.
Month 3Read the weekly review honestly.Most changes T0. Repeated NO-DATA treated as a workflow problem rather than a tool problem. Flip conditions occasionally met.

That second sentence in week two is the entire control. It is why --strict moves only by a human editing the workflow file, where a reviewer sees it in the diff, and why a session instruction can never waive a hard gate.

Try it on the change you would be most embarrassed to get wrong.

Keeping this booklet true

Four tables in this act are generated from the repository. Rebuild them at every loop close, and let CI catch you when you forget.

sbe book            # regenerate every derived table
sbe book --check    # does any bound source disagree with this booklet

Under --strict in CI, a bound source that moved without a rebuild is a FAIL naming the section and the file. A stale prose stamp is NO-DATA: it reports that nobody has reread the words, and it never claims the words are wrong, because no tool can compute that.
