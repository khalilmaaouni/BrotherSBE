Tab three

How does the work get shared out when a machine writes most of the code?

Nine pieces, most of them pictures. Who owns which decision, where AI workers are allowed to write, who is allowed to challenge them, and what has to exist on disk before anybody merges. Framework first, then one real change walked end to end.

The promise this tab has to earn is narrow and specific: your senior people stop being the standing verification layer, and stay the people who decide.

Teams that ship with AI keep arriving at the same shape by accident. Work is generated quickly, and then it queues behind the two or three people who can tell whether it is true. Nothing in that arrangement is written down, so nobody can change it. The queue is invisible until somebody goes on holiday.

What follows is the arrangement written down. One governed workflow from intent to proof, with three mechanisms holding it up: design before code, own work before parallelism, evidence before done. Read the diagrams first. The prose is there to argue with them.

Where the chain is held together by hand, said now rather than later

There is no tracker integration. None, of any kind. Stage one happens in whatever system your team already uses, and the product cannot see it. The link from a ticket to a design directory is a line somebody types, and if they stop typing it the chain breaks in silence: nothing detects a missing link, and nothing reports a broken one. Everything downstream reads a git diff, a JSON file or a markdown file, so anything that never becomes one of those three is invisible here.

Who does what, when a machine is holding the keyboard

This is the picture the rest of the tab exists to support. Seven lanes, one change, left to right.

A reviewer that can edit will fix what it finds, and a finding fixed in silence is a finding nobody recorded.

The separation is declared rather than merely instructed: every specialist reviewer role is registered with Read, Grep, Glob, Bash and nothing else, and exactly one role in the register holds Edit and Write. That role writes code and reviews nothing. Be exact about the strength of it: Bash is in that reviewer set, so this is a role boundary a host honours, not a sandbox that makes writing impossible.

How do you run three workers at once without a merge nobody can unpick?

Parallelism is where small teams lose their nerve, and they are right to. Two agents editing the same file at the same time produces a merge you cannot reason about, and the cost lands on the person who has to unpick it.

The practical effect on a small team is that scope stops being a conversation. A brief names the paths in scope, the paths that must not be touched, the dependencies, the acceptance criteria, the commands that count as verification, the constraints already known, and the conditions on which to stop rather than improvise. Two runs of the same brief produce byte-identical JSON, and a brief that would exceed its size limit is refused rather than truncated, because a silently shortened brief is a scope change nobody agreed to.

From a business outcome to a merge decision, and back again

Who reviews this change, and why it is not a matter of opinion

No model decides which specialist reviews the change. The diff does.

The order is fixed and unarguable: migration, then security, then data, then backend, then infrastructure, then QA, then evidence. At most two reviewers are ever selected, and how many slots exist depends only on the tier. The router reads the diff, the intake file when one is discoverable, and the evidence store when one exists, and nothing else: no clock, no random source, no model call. Run it twice on the same two commits and you get the same answer, which is the property that lets a person argue with it.

Ownership moves when somebody accepts it, not when somebody sends it

The record derives what it can and refuses to invent the rest. Done, in flight and not started come from the plan compared against the task registry. Active tasks carry their worktree and base commit. Every worktree is checked for uncommitted work, and every evidence item is tagged current, stale or unavailable. Open questions, decisions and required access are written empty on purpose, because the tool cannot know them and a plausible guess in that field is worse than a blank one.

If you adopted nothing else, which five rules would change a week?

Everything above compresses into five sentences. If your team adopted nothing else, these five would still change how a week runs.

That last rule is the one that changes meetings. When someone says a change is done, the reply is no longer a judgment about their diligence. It is a question with a mechanical answer: which check, run against which commit, over which paths, exiting with what code, taking how long.

One change, walked end to end

A price-bearing field on the product master is retyped, and its unit meaning changes. Three systems read it: the order service, the cart service and the search index. The design happens in one timezone and the build happens in another.

This is the ordinary case, not the dramatic one. Nothing here is an outage. It is one field, three consumers, two working days that do not overlap, and a set of seams where work is usually lost.

Day one, morning, the design site

A retailer sees the wrong case pack price. The account team establishes which customers and which surfaces are affected, and what the workaround is. None of this touches the product: it has no tracker integration and cannot see any of it.

The only discipline available at this seam is a convention the team keeps: a request states an outcome and a blast radius before an analyst picks it up.

The seam. Request to design. What crosses. A ticket description, and later the same words transcribed into the purpose file. The link from that ticket to the design directory is a line somebody types by hand, and a broken link gives no signal at all.

Day one, late morning

The analyst runs the intake with the commercial requester in the room, because they are the only people who can answer the money question. Yes to money computes the top tier, which owes all seven artifacts. No would compute the bottom tier, which owes nothing, and at that moment nothing contradicts a wrong answer. That is the honest shape of it: the rule is unarguable, and the inputs are five sentences a human typed.

Purpose, process and verification get written while the people who know the intent are awake. The process artifact names an actor, a trigger and an exception path for every step, and the exception states are named across all three surfaces: a stale price, a partial sync, an index behind the database, a voucher service unreachable while a discount stacks.

Be exact about what is checked here. Nothing reads purpose, process or verification for content. The design checks verify that the file exists and is no longer the shipped template. A one-sentence purpose brief passes.

Day one, afternoon

The integration lead writes down which store owns the field, once, for real: which system is the system of record, which tables are a replica, which index is derived, and which store owns editorial copy and not price. Every relationship carries a cardinality, including the one that traps this business, where a variant to a stock unit to a contract price list is many to many and gets implemented as one to many by whoever writes the sync first.

One decision record says why this store and not that one, with two genuinely distinct rejected alternatives and a flip condition. The second analyst then refutes the dossier rather than approving it, and reconciles the declared tier against what the diff actually touches.

The check that names the field, and the check that cannot know the answer

The data model check fails an entity with no named system of record and a relationship with no cardinality. It cannot tell a correct answer from a confident wrong one: naming the editorial store where the truth is the product master passes every check that ships. The same holds for the decision record, where the shape is checked and the judgment is not. This is why stage four is a second human reading, and why no amount of tooling removes it.

Day one, evening. The timezone seam, outbound

The plan is derived and one work brief is minted per task: the export and the sync mapping, the order and cart contracts and their consumers, the index mapping and reindex, and the two front ends.

Ownership is prepared at a specific commit and does not move until a named engineer on the receiving side runs acknowledge. Reject is available, and leaves the work where it was.

The seam. Day to night. What crosses. One byte-identical brief per task going out, carrying scope, must-not-touch, dependencies, acceptance, verification commands, known constraints, stop conditions and the baseline commit. What still does not cross. Nothing notifies anybody. The people who can answer an intent question are asleep for the whole build window. A stop condition converts an eight-hour stall into a written escalation, and nothing converts it into an answer.

Day one, night, the build site

The night engineers work against the dossier instead of a chat scrollback. Evidence is minted by the runner that executed the command and never typed: base and head commit, redacted arguments, exit code, duration, and content digests of the declared covered paths. The migration receipt records the forward and the reverse leg, both run against a restored copy, with matching row counts. A stop condition becomes a recorded stop and a written open question rather than eight hours of guessing.

Receipts minted on a laptop are labelled local and advisory. The label is part of the receipt, so nobody has to remember which machine produced it.

Day two, morning. The timezone seam, inbound

The night shift prepares the return handover at a specific commit. The lead acknowledges, or rejects with a named reason such as a consumer that was not updated. Acknowledge refuses outright on a stale record and names both commits, and a receiver who reads as the outgoing owner is refused at both ends.

What still does not cross. Nothing stops the lead merging while ignoring the record entirely. The record makes the state honest. Your branch rules are what make it binding.

Day two, the merge path

The reviewers are routed from the diff, not chosen. Findings come back as findings. The gates read the receipts. Convergence compares the diff against the approved dossier along scope, contracts, data, architecture and verification, and reports the file types it could not read as unmeasured rather than as a verdict, which is the difference between a limit and a lie. A human merges.

What this arrangement does not do, stated where a buyer can find it

It does not shrink a timezone. It blocks nothing on its own: every gate is a command that prints a verdict, and your own branch rules and CI configuration sit between a failing verdict and a stopped merge. It cannot see absent evidence, only declared evidence, so a change that declares nothing produces NO-DATA lines rather than a failure. It reads no ticketing system, no admin screen and no telemetry, so anything that never becomes a diff, a JSON file or a markdown file is invisible to it. And a complete dossier written after the code passes every check identically, which is why stage four is a person and not a script.

The point is not that the machine is trusted. It is that trust stops being a personal favour your senior engineer does for the team every week.
