For your role

What changes in the seat you actually sit in

Six seats, each written the same way: what hurts, what is different on Monday morning, which decisions are yours, what you hand over and receive, what is genuinely checked for you and what is not, and one change small enough to try this week. On a team of four, somebody is holding two of these seats at once.

Start with the seat that carries the others. If AI is writing more of your engineering, the constraint is not how fast the work gets written. It is how fast one senior person can decide whether it is true.

Two programmes appear throughout, and they never share a merge path. The integration and analytics programme runs on Azure Data Factory and SAP Integration Suite, landing into Databricks and Snowflake, with Azure DevOps repositories and pipelines. The services programme runs .NET microservices on AWS, with Bitbucket and Jenkins. Where a control differs between them, both are named. Nothing written for one applies to the other.

Staff engineer, principal engineer or hands-on tech lead

Stop being the human checksum for every AI-generated change

Your judgement is the scarcest thing on the team, and it is currently being spent re-reading work rather than shaping it.

The pressure

AI multiplies implementation throughput. It does not multiply review capacity, and review capacity is you. So the queue behind you grows in exactly the way a queue grows when arrivals speed up and the server does not.

Four things hurt, and they compound.

What actually happensWhy it does not get better on its own
Every change that matters eventually reaches youYou are the routing rule of last resort, and there is no written rule to compete with you
You reconstruct the same architecture context, weeklyThe reasoning lives in your head and in closed pull request threads, which nothing indexes
Your standards travel as review commentsA comment persuades one author once. It is not present the next time an agent writes the same shape
You are pulled in after the expensive decisionGrain, boundary and contract were settled before there was a diff for you to read

A standard that exists only in your review comments is not a standard. It is your attention, rented out one pull request at a time.

What changes on Monday

Nothing philosophical. Four concrete differences, all visible in the repository.

  • The tier is computed before anyone opens an editor. Five intake questions, first match wins, T0 to T3. A T0 change owes nothing and does not arrive on your desk carrying a dossier. A T3 change that has cleared the design check arrives with its decisions already written down, because the check FAILs otherwise.
  • The decision you have explained three times becomes a file. 03-adr.md carries the decision, two genuinely distinct rejected alternatives, and the flip condition that would reopen it. The design check FAILs an ADR missing any of those.
  • Reviewer selection stops being you by default. sbe review-route reads the diff and names at most two specialist reviewers, deterministically, with no model choosing. Zero is a legal result rather than a claim that a review happened. It names specialist roles and adds nobody to a pull request, so who is actually asked stays your team's rule.
  • Your standard becomes a registered check with a receipt, or it stays a comment. The check registry names the executable, its exact argument vector, its working directory, the files it is evidence for, and the runner files whose bytes are the check.

The one that will bite you

A dossier written after the code passes every check identically. The order is a discipline that no tool computes and no pipeline can enforce across days. If the team stops writing dossiers first, every verdict degrades to NO-DATA, and NO-DATA does not block. That is the failure mode to watch for in month two, not month one.

Backend engineer

Change a contract without hearing about it from the partner

Your expensive mistakes are a boundary drawn in the wrong place and a contract changed without knowing who consumed it.

The pressure

Claude will happily change an endpoint's response shape. It cannot know that a partner batch job parses that response at 2am, because nobody wrote that down anywhere it could read. You find out from the partner.

The same shape recurs inside the estate. On the services programme a price-bearing field is published by one system, synchronised into a service database, read by an order service and a cart service, and derived into a search index. Four consumers, three of them invisible in the diff that changes the field.

What changes on Monday

The intake asks whether this changes a contract others depend on and how many break if it is wrong. Answer yes and many and you are at T2, owing six artifacts, one of which is the decision record with its flip condition.

The flip condition is the field teams skip, and it is the one that pays.

Revisit toward a split if a second team comes to need independent deployment of the pricing path, or if the consistency requirement moves from eventual to strict for order totals.

Neither is true today: one team deploys this, and Finance reconciles daily rather than continuously.

Six months later that paragraph is the difference between revisiting a decision and re-litigating it from nothing.

The second change is the exception path. The process artifact asks for it by name, and on service work the exception path is the document. "On a timeout the order stays in pending_payment and a reconciliation job resolves it within fifteen minutes, never retried inline, because the provider is not idempotent on this endpoint without an idempotency key and we do not send one today" is the sentence that settles, before the timeout happens, what the system does instead of charging twice. It exists because a template demanded it, and nothing reads it afterwards: it is worth writing because a human reads it, not because a check does.

My lane

  • Process, in 02-process.md: an actor, a trigger and an exception path per step, and the consumers named in prose because nothing enumerates them for you.
  • The contract: the response shape, its version, and the compatibility window during which both shapes are served.
  • The boundary: which service owns the behaviour, recorded in the ADR when it moves.
  • The rollout order: publisher before consumer, or consumer before publisher, decided rather than discovered.

My handoffs

  • You receive 00-intake.json and 01-purpose.md from the Technical BA, and 05-data-model.md naming which store owns the field you are about to change.
  • You give 02-process.md with its exception paths to QA, who turn them into negative cases; the contract change to whoever owns each consumer; and the migration and its reverse to the platform seat.
  • You receive back the reviewer routing verdict, the compatibility evidence, and on a rebase a regenerated receipt, because a receipt minted on a feature branch tip FAILs at the merge commit under linear history.

My controls

Your reviewer. backend-reviewer, read-only, covering contract compatibility, idempotency, concurrency, transaction boundaries, error paths, retries, observability and performance. Touch a secret, an authentication or authorization surface, a payment, a partner or personal data, and security-reviewer outranks it: the fixed order is migration, then security, then data, then backend. At T2 and T3 two slots exist, so both can be named. At T0 and T1 there is one slot, so the higher-priority role takes it and the one it displaced is reported as unmeasured rather than quietly dropped.

Reviewer selection is deterministic: sbe review-route reads the diff, no model chooses, at most two specialists, and zero is a legal result rather than a claim of a clean review.

What is not checked for you, stated precisely

There is no consumer impact analysis and no deploy-ordering check between a publisher and its consumer. The impact detectors read path names, not code: the money detector matches pricing, discount and invoice, and not the bare word price, so a file named PriceList.json raises the tier floor to T2 and no further. The silent-failure linter reads .py, .sql, .swift, .rb, .js, .ts and .go. There is no .cs, so on the services programme the .NET services, the queue processors and the cron jobs are outside it, and no configuration closes that.

My diagram

Try it on: my first change

Before you touch the next endpoint, list its consumers by name in 02-process.md. Then ask the tool what it can see from the paths alone.

Know which number is right, and be able to show why

Every failure in your work is silent by construction. A wrong number is formatted exactly like a right one.

Two SAP ingestion facts that outrank any tooling choice

Microsoft states that a security patch announced in SAP Note 3255746 blocks incoming calls to the ODP RFC API from third-party clients, including the SAP CDC connector in Azure Data Factory. Of the four replacements Microsoft names, only some have shipped, so confirm the current position against Microsoft Learn and against SAP's own note system before planning around either.

Second, the SAP CDC Checkpoint Key is per source. Share one between two pipelines and one overwrites the other's change-capture state while both keep succeeding. The property is hidden entirely when run mode is set to full on every run, so flipping run mode silently discards checkpoint identity.

The ceiling on the numbers gate, printed on every run

The gate proves that two derivations differ in text beyond case, whitespace, comments and trailing punctuation, and that two transcribed figures match. It executes no query and has no database connectivity, so the figures are transcribed by a human. Two counts taken downstream of the same broken synchronisation agree, and the gate passes them. It is a floor worth having and it is worth knowing the height of.

QA engineer

Decide what would prove this wrong, before anyone writes it

Your problem is not writing tests. It is that a green suite and a correct system are two different claims, and only one of them is being made.

The pressure

An agent writes tests alongside the code, and they pass. Of course they pass: they were written from the same understanding that produced the code. A test that encodes the same misunderstanding as the implementation is worse than no test, because it converts an open question into a settled one.

This is why the seat moves. Arriving after implementation, the strongest thing you can do is find defects. Arriving before it, you decide what would count as proof, and that decision is worth more than any number of cases written afterwards.

What changes on Monday

  • At the release decision you give the readiness summary: blockers first, then what was never examined.

My controls

What fires for you. The ran gate, which is blunter than it sounds and more useful than it sounds. Each recorded check must name what ran, exit zero, and take a nonzero duration. A check that took no time did not run.

Underneath it sits a defect worth understanding, because it is the reason the evidence layer is shaped the way it is.

A receipt used to prove that a process started, was timed, and exited. So true minted a clean, sealed, commit-bound receipt.

The command ran. That was provable.

The command was the check this change owed. That was not.

Now the check registry names the executable, its exact argument vector, its working directory, the files it is evidence for, and the runner files whose bytes are the check. sbe evidence run --check <id> resolves all of that from the registry, and there is no argument on that path that changes what runs.

Your reviewer. qa-reviewer maps requirements and acceptance criteria to executable tests, finds missing negative and non-functional coverage, and validates that the test evidence says what it is claimed to say.

Three ceilings on your evidence, in plain terms

The recorded trust level tops out at CI-CLAIMED, which the module's own text defines as CI-shaped metadata that nothing verified. A laptop exporting the same run identifier on a clean tree receives the same label, and the seal is a checksum rather than a signature. Second, a receipt is commit-bound: under linear history a receipt minted before a rebase FAILs at the merge commit, so the pipeline has to regenerate it on the post-rebase head, and the receipt must be written outside the tree it covers or it makes that tree dirty. Third, nothing detects that a change needed a check and declared none: the absence returns NO-DATA, and NO-DATA does not block.

The rule that changes your review verdicts. A verdict counts only when it names the falsification actually executed: the command re-run, the defect reproduced, the number re-derived. Reasoning alone is NO-DATA, not a finding.

That rule is graded at the weekly review and nothing parses a verdict, so it is a discipline rather than a control. It is listed among the honest limits for exactly that reason.

Technical BA

You own the intent every downstream gate inherits

Every tier, every artifact, every check and every acceptance row is computed from what you write first. Nothing below can correct it.

The pressure

The business asks for something in its own words. By the time it reaches a schema, three translations have happened and nobody recorded any of them. When the delivered thing is wrong, the argument is about what was asked for, and there is no artifact to settle it.

And here is the honest limit, stated second because it is second in importance rather than first: nothing reads your prose for content. The design checks verify that the file is present and that it is not still the shipped template. A one-sentence purpose brief passes. There is no gate that can rescue a vague success criterion, which is precisely why the criterion has to be right when you write it.

The gate checks that you wrote something. Only you check that it is the real problem.

Platform engineer

Make the rollback a thing that has run, not a thing that reads correct

You are the one who has to believe the rollback, and you are the one holding the pager when it turns out nobody tested it.

The pressure

A migration that reads correct is not a migration that has been run backwards on real data. Expand and contract compressed into one release is a reliable way to make a rollback impossible, and it looks completely fine in review.

The second pressure is quieter. You are the person who has to turn a printed verdict into something that actually stops a merge, and that wiring is yours in both programmes. Nothing here ships it.

What changes on Monday

The sequence that survives.

  1. Expand. Add the new column nullable, deploy, write to both.
  2. Backfill, with the reconciliation registered as a check and run through the evidence wrapper.
  3. Read from the new column, deploy.
  4. Contract. Drop the old column, in a later release, only once the previous one is no longer deployable.

Step four in the same release as step one is the failure. migration-reviewer covers exactly this: forward and reverse evidence, expand and contract compatibility, lock duration, mixed-schema deployment and rollback time.

And the migration gate wants a receipt rather than a document: both legs run against a restored copy, a rehearsal identifier, and row counts that match. A receipt with no row counts is NO-DATA, and NO-DATA is never a pass.

My lane

  • The rehearsal: a restored copy at production-like volume, forward and reverse, emitting the receipt.
  • The pipeline stages: which command runs where, in what order, and which are advisory rather than blocking this month.
  • The enforcement wiring: the merge rule and the branch permission that convert a nonzero exit into a stopped merge.
  • The incident path: decided in writing before an incident, because a gate that blocks an emergency fix gets waived once and then quietly deleted.

My handoffs

  • You receive the migration and its reverse from the data and backend seats, and the registered check identifiers from QA.
  • You give the pipeline stages and their verdict blocks back to everyone, plus the evidence pack assembled immediately before a human approval: the team status and the re-run gate verdicts, bound to the release commit and written where the approver will actually read them.
  • You give a regenerated receipt on the post-rebase head, because a pre-rebase receipt FAILs at the merge commit under linear history.

My controls

Where the blast radius rule sits with you. BrotherSBE never gets apply rights on production state. It drafts the exact command with its rollback and hands it to a human. Credentials are never typed, stored or logged.

On a T0 change it costs five questions and owes nothing. That is the common case, and it is meant to be.

On a T2 or T3 change the cost is real: six or seven artifacts, each a piece of thinking somebody has to do. The argument is not that this is free. It is that you were going to pay for those decisions anyway, and design time is cheaper than incident time.

If your changes are almost never T2 or T3, you probably do not need this. That is a legitimate answer and nothing here is going to argue you out of it.
