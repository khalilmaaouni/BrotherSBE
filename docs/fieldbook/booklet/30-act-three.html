Act three

Five seats at the table

On a small team one person often holds two of these. Find the seat you actually sit in: the pain in your own words, the gate that fires for you, and what changes on Monday.

Seat one

Backend engineer

Your expensive mistakes are a boundary drawn in the wrong place and a contract changed without knowing who consumed it.

The pain. Claude will happily change an endpoint's response shape. It cannot know that a partner batch job parses that response at 2am, because nobody wrote that down anywhere it could read. You find out from the partner.

What fires for you. The intake asks whether this changes a contract others depend on and how many consumers break if it is wrong. Answer yes and many and you are at T2, owing six artifacts, one of which is a decision record with two genuinely distinct rejected alternatives and a flip condition.

The part that pays. The flip condition, which is the field teams skip.

Revisit toward a split if a second team comes to need independent deployment of the pricing path, or if the consistency requirement moves from eventual to strict for order totals.

Neither is true today: one team deploys this, and Finance reconciles daily rather than continuously.

Six months later that paragraph is the difference between revisiting a decision and re-litigating it from nothing.

On the exception path. The process artifact asks for it by name, and on service work the exception path is the document. "On a timeout the order stays in pending_payment and a reconciliation job resolves it within fifteen minutes, never retried inline, because the provider is not idempotent on this endpoint without an idempotency key and we do not send one today" is the sentence that prevents a double charge. It exists because a template demanded it.

Your reviewer. backend-reviewer, read-only, covering contract compatibility, idempotency, concurrency, transaction boundaries, error paths, retries, observability and performance. Touch auth, money, partner APIs, file upload, secrets or personal data and security-reviewer joins it automatically.

Reviewer selection is deterministic: sbe review-route reads the diff, no model chooses, at most two specialists, and zero is a legal result rather than a claim of a clean review.

Seat three

Infrastructure engineer

You are the one who has to believe the rollback, and you are the one holding the pager when it turns out nobody tested it.

The pain. A migration that reads correct is not a migration that has been run backwards on real data. Expand and contract compressed into one release is the single most common way a rollback becomes impossible, and it looks completely fine in review.

The sequence that survives.

  1. Expand. Add the new column nullable, deploy, write to both.
  2. Backfill, with the reconciliation registered as a check and run through the evidence wrapper.
  3. Read from the new column, deploy.
  4. Contract. Drop the old column, in a later release, only once the previous one is no longer deployable.

Step four in the same release as step one is the failure. migration-reviewer covers exactly this: forward and reverse evidence, expand and contract compatibility, lock duration, mixed-schema deployment and rollback time.

Where the blast radius rule sits with you. BrotherSBE never gets apply rights on production state. It drafts the exact command with its rollback and hands it to a human. Credentials are never typed, stored or logged.

Say this out loud before you adopt it

That rule is a stated discipline plus whatever access control your estate already has. Nothing in this plugin can revoke a credential your shell already holds. If your real requirement is that an agent physically cannot deploy, that is your CI permissions and your cloud IAM, not a line in a skill file.

What you must supply yourself. No CODEOWNERS and no branch protection ships here. The CI workflow guards nothing until you copy it into your own repository, and --strict moves only by a human editing that workflow, visible in the diff. That last property is deliberate: a session instruction can never waive a hard gate.

Seat four

QA engineer

Your problem is not writing tests. It is that a green suite and a correct system are two different claims, and only one of them is being made.

The pain. An agent writes tests alongside the code, and they pass. Of course they pass: they were written from the same understanding that produced the code. A test that encodes the same misunderstanding as the implementation is worse than no test, because it converts an open question into a settled one.

What fires for you. The ran gate, which is blunter than it sounds and more useful than it sounds. Each recorded check must name what ran, exit zero, and take a nonzero duration. A check that took no time did not run.

Underneath it sits a defect worth understanding, because it is the reason the evidence layer is shaped the way it is.

A receipt used to prove that a process started, was timed, and exited. So -- true minted a clean, sealed, commit-bound receipt.

The command ran. That was provable.

The command was the check this change owed. That was not.

Now the check registry names the executable, its exact argument vector, its working directory, the files it is evidence for, and the runner files whose bytes are the check. sbe evidence run --check <id> resolves all of that from the registry, and there is no argument on that path that changes what runs.

Your reviewer. qa-reviewer maps requirements and acceptance criteria to executable tests, finds missing negative and non-functional coverage, and validates that the test evidence says what it is claimed to say. On interface and service work the missing coverage is almost always the failure path: the network error, the declined card, the expired session.

The rule that changes your review verdicts. A verdict counts only when it names the falsification actually executed: the command re-run, the defect reproduced, the number re-derived. Reasoning alone is NO-DATA, not a finding.

That rule is graded at the weekly review and nothing parses a verdict, so it is a discipline rather than a control. It is listed among the honest limits for exactly that reason.

Seat five

Technical BA

You are the only person in the room whose output has no gate at all, and that is precisely why the design phase was built.

The pain. The business asks for something in its own words. By the time it reaches a schema, three translations have happened and nobody recorded any of them. When the delivered thing is wrong, the argument is about what was asked for, and there is no artifact to settle it.

Where you own the document. The purpose brief is yours, and it has four parts that each stop a specific argument.

SectionThe argument it prevents
Problem, stated with no solution in itBuilding the first idea somebody had instead of the thing that is broken
Users, and what they do today insteadSolving for a user who does not exist
Success criteria, observable"It feels faster" as an acceptance criterion
Non-goals, explicitThe scope creep that turns a fix into a quarter of work
What breaks if this is wrongDiscovering the blast radius during the incident

The non-goals section earns its place fastest. "This does not change how revenue is recognised" is one line, and it is the line that stops a reporting fix becoming an accounting project.

Observable, concretely. "A user can complete checkout without leaving the page, and a failed payment leaves no orphaned order" is checkable by somebody who was not in the room. "The checkout feels faster" is not, and no gate downstream can rescue it.

What you get back. The verification artifact turns your acceptance criteria into a table of one row per claim, the check that proves it, and when it runs. A claim with no check is listed as having none, rather than omitted, which is the difference between a plan and a wish.

On a T0 change it costs five questions and owes nothing. That is the common case, and it is meant to be.

On a T2 or T3 change the cost is real: six or seven artifacts, each a piece of thinking somebody has to do. The argument is not that this is free. It is that you were going to pay for those decisions anyway, and design time is cheaper than incident time.

If your changes are almost never T2 or T3, you probably do not need this. That is a legitimate answer and nothing here is going to argue you out of it.
